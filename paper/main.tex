\begin{abstract}
We populate a continuous double auction with traders whose decisions are made
by a typed language model that returns a probability distribution over actions,
and vary only how that distribution is consumed. Taking the model's modal
action---a common deployment choice---makes the market stop trading after
shocks: following a positive shock, the market clears in $65.6\%$ of periods
against $91.3\%$ after a negative shock, a gap of $25.8$ percentage points
(95\% CI $[17.3, 34.2]$, $t=6.37$, $n=20$, preregistered). Sampling from the
same distribution reduces the gap to $3.1$ points. Two algorithmic baselines
that are symmetric by construction show no gap.

The mechanism is a single one. Every trader conditions on the same public
signal, so a decoder that takes the mode of one stored response makes them act
identically, and a market of
unanimous buyers has no counterparty. Measured at the shock itself, $90.0\%$ of
traders take the same side after a positive shock against $59.4\%$ after a
negative one, and $100\%$ of non-trading periods under modal decoding had one
side of the order book entirely empty, against $3.4\%$ for the
zero-intelligence baseline.

We test that mechanism with a preregistered prediction it could have failed: if
unanimity is the cause, the effect must weaken sharply as the market grows.
It does,
from $37.0$ points at four traders to $4.2$ at thirty-two ($-0.112$ per
doubling, seed-paired $t=-7.78$). A companion prediction---that sampling would
show no such decay---is \emph{unresolved}: the seed-paired estimate is
$t=-2.00$, two-sided $p=0.060$, which neither falsifies it nor, absent a
preregistered equivalence margin, confirms it.

Separately, we find that ordinary domain wording induces a $25$-point
asymmetry in the model's stated conviction between buying and selling at equal
mispricing, which vanishes under mirror-image phrasing. A preregistered test
found this does \emph{not} move pricing error, and we report that null.

Every confirmatory hypothesis test was preregistered on seeds disjoint from
those that generated it; the wording study and the order-book diagnostics are
exploratory and labelled as such. Of ten registered predictions, four were
confirmed (D1, D2, E1, G1), four failed (C1, C2, F1, and the original clause
that modal decoding would be immune), one is unresolved (E2) and one is
reported as not identified at the sample size reached (F2). A separate
post-hoc mechanism account is withdrawn and identified as such. All are
reported, including a disclosed deviation in which F1 and G1 were first run on
the wrong population and reanalysed.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A language model asked to choose among actions returns, in principle, a
distribution. A common deployment choice is to discard it and take the mode.
For a single decision this selects the most probable action, which is correct
under 0--1 loss against the returned distribution. This paper
asks what happens when many agents driven by the same model make related
decisions at the same time.

The answer, in a market, is that the market stops. Modal decoding makes agents
conditioning on shared information act identically. In a setting that requires
counterparties, unanimity is not consensus; it is the absence of trade. We
measure this in a continuous double auction, isolate the mechanism, and confirm
it with a prediction that could have failed and did not.

Our contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A liquidity failure caused by decoding, not by reasoning.} The
  model's modal action is \emph{correct} on both sides at every mispricing in
  our static probe grid, and at the shock itself in the market it takes the
  correct side in both directions. The failure is in the aggregation of
  decisions that are individually defensible.
\item \textbf{A mechanism measured in the market.} We instrument both sides of
  the order book and show that under modal decoding, non-trading periods lack a
  counterparty essentially always, while for symmetric baselines they do not.
  Agreement at the shock is itself directional---$90.0\%$ after a positive
  shock against $59.4\%$ after a negative one---so unanimity accounts for both
  the magnitude of the halt and which shock it strikes. An earlier draft
  posited a second component; Section~\ref{par:two} explains why that was an
  artefact of reading a static probe as a market statistic, and withdraws it.
\item \textbf{A falsifiable size-scaling test.} The mechanism implies the
  effect must decay with market size at a rate set by the agreement rate. We
  registered this before running it; it held.
\item \textbf{A prompt-sensitivity result, and a null.} Domain-idiomatic
  wording induces a large conviction asymmetry invisible to anyone reading only
  the modal action. A preregistered test found it does not move pricing error.
\end{enumerate}

We emphasise the failures, and state the registration record exactly.

\textbf{Four preregistered predictions failed.} C1, that the wording asymmetry
would move pricing error (a clean null, Section~\ref{sec:wording}); the
original clause that modal decoding would be immune to the liquidity effect;
C2, that the sampling arm's wording contrast would be \emph{smaller} than the
argmax arm's, on which the estimate came back the wrong sign ($+0.161$,
$p=0.80$); and F1, that the modal action is stable enough across repeated live
calls to certify the policy as effectively deterministic
(Section~\ref{sec:estimand}).

\textbf{Four were confirmed}: D1, D2, E1 and G1. \textbf{One is unresolved}: E2
(Section~\ref{sec:size}). \textbf{One is not identified} at the sample size we
reached: F2, whose claim-aligned form is discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:estimand}.

Separately, and not part of the registration record, we withdraw a
\emph{post-hoc} two-component account of the mechanism that an earlier draft
proposed (Section~\ref{par:two}). The preregistration for that run deliberately
instrumented rather than predicted, so this was never a registered hypothesis
and we do not count it as one. The result that survived did so against a test
designed to kill it.

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Market design and baselines.} The experimental market tradition
begins with \citet{smith1962}. Our lower baseline is the zero-intelligence
trader of \citet{gode1993}, whose result---that budget-constrained random
traders achieve high allocative efficiency---makes a random arm a genuine floor
rather than a strawman, and motivates the value clamp that prevents any arm in
our design from knowingly trading at a loss.

\paragraph{LLM agents in simulated markets.} \citet{lopezlira2025} builds a
simulated stock market with a persistent order book in which heterogeneous LLM
agents submit structured decisions, and reports that agents adhere consistently
to assigned strategies while the market reproduces price discovery, bubbles and
underreaction. That work also notes that prompts can generate correlated
behaviours affecting market stability, which is the phenomenon we isolate and
measure here.

\paragraph{Correlation as systemic risk.} Closest to our result is
\citet{ross2026}, who argue that shared training and architecture make more
capable models behave more similarly, creating a non-diversifiable risk floor,
and who test this with LLM traders of varying capability in an agent-based
market. They find correlated behaviour that \emph{increases} with capability,
and that it becomes a liability when agents share a misinformation
environment.

Our contribution is complementary and, we think, orthogonal in a useful way.
They vary the model and attribute correlation to properties of the model---its
training, its architecture, its capability. We hold the model \emph{fixed} and
vary only how its output distribution is consumed. The correlation we measure
is therefore not solely a property of the model but also of a deployment
choice, and that component a practitioner controls directly: our argmax and sampling arms
issue identical requests and read identical responses, differing only in a
single line of client code. Where \citet{ross2026} identify a risk floor set by
model similarity, we show a large component of realised correlation sitting
above that floor, substantially reduced here without changing models---though
sampling still leaves a $3.1$-point gap, so the decoder does not account for
all of it.

\paragraph{Herding and cascades.} The classical account of correlated action is
the informational cascade of \citet{bikhchandani1992}, in which an agent
rationally ignores private information after observing enough predecessors.
Our mechanism is deliberately simpler and should not be confused with it.
Agents in our market do not observe one another's decisions and draw no
inference from them; they condition on a common public signal and a
deterministic policy maps that signal to the same action. There is no learning,
no sequence, and no inference---only a degenerate policy. The resulting
unanimity looks like herding and has the same market consequence, but it
arises without any of the cascade machinery, which is why it can be switched
off at the decoder.

\paragraph{Decoding and diversity.} That greedy decoding collapses output
diversity is long established in text generation: \citet{holtzman2020} show
that maximisation-based decoding yields repetitive, degenerate text and
introduce nucleus sampling as a remedy. The quality--diversity tension is
well understood \emph{within} a single generation. We report a consequence one
level up: when many agents share a decoder, the diversity that sampling
preserves is not a stylistic property of one output but the raw material a
market needs to clear. Sampling is individually worse and collectively
necessary.

\paragraph{Confidence and calibration.} \citet{kadavath2022} study whether
language models can predict the correctness of their own answers, and
subsequent work has documented systematic overconfidence in verbalised
confidence. Our Experiment~\ref{sec:wording} touches this literature from an
unusual angle: the model we use returns a probability distribution natively
rather than a verbalised estimate, and we find that ordinary domain wording
shifts that distribution by 25 points without changing the selected option.
Any calibration measurement taken over such a model inherits whatever asymmetry
the question's phrasing introduced.

\paragraph{What we could not find.} We did not find prior work isolating the
decode rule as a cause of market-level liquidity failure, nor a preregistered
size-scaling test of a herding mechanism in an LLM-populated market. We would
welcome pointers; this survey was conducted by the authors and is not
exhaustive.

\section{The instrument}

\subsection{Market}

A continuous double auction in one abstract good. Prices are integer ticks;
matching is price-then-time priority; an incoming order executes at the resting
order's price. A trader's resting orders are withdrawn at the top of its turn,
so each trader has at most one live order. Submitting on one side cancels the
trader's resting orders on the other, preventing wash trades without leaving a
crossed book.

Accounts are conserved by construction. Each trade moves cash from buyer to
seller and inventory from seller to buyer, one for one. Short sales and
borrowing are disallowed, and every order is pre-checked against uncommitted
cash or uncommitted inventory. A property test submits 20{,}000 random orders
and asserts after every one that total cash and total units are exactly their
endowed values, that no account is negative, that no trader has committed more
than it holds, and that the book is never crossed.

Order size is fixed at one unit, so aggressiveness is the only lever an arm
has; position sizing is a second channel and is deliberately disabled.

\subsection{Fundamental and information}

The fundamental $F_t$ is piecewise constant. For the size and liquidity
experiments we use a \emph{matched-jump} process: jumps are evenly spaced, of
identical magnitude, strictly alternating in direction, and even in number, so
that every up window is paired with a down window of the same size between the
same two price levels. This makes the up-versus-down comparison within-run and
matched, which matters: under a random-jump process the per-run noise on this
statistic has standard deviation $0.74$, large enough that a single seed of the
symmetric baseline returned a spurious $-0.82$ gap.

Each trader's private value is $F_t + \varepsilon_{i,t}$ with
$\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 5)$, redrawn each period. All experiments here
use full information; the delayed and noisy signal treatments are implemented
but not reported.

\subsection{The decision contract}

Code owns matching, budgets, order sizes and prices. An agent answers three
typed questions and nothing else: direction (\texttt{buy}/\texttt{sell}/%
\texttt{pass}), urgency on a five-level scale, and whether the signal is
already reflected in the book. A code-owned rule maps urgency to an integer
tick, anchored on the book and hard-clamped at the agent's own private value.

That clamp is load-bearing. \emph{An agent cannot express a losing price},
because it never expresses a price. A 20{,}000-case property test asserts that
no quote is ever on the wrong side of the quoting agent's value. Consequently
every effect we report is about direction and timing, never about arithmetic.

The question wording is frozen and hash-locked; a test fails if any instruction
or criterion changes. This is the defence against unrecorded prompt iteration.

\subsection{Arms}

All four arms emit the same object, including a distribution over
$\{\texttt{buy}, \texttt{sell}, \texttt{pass}\}$, which makes them comparable.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{ZI}: random direction and urgency, fixed policy distribution,
  value-clamped so it never knowingly trades at a loss.
\item \textbf{NBR}: logit best-response over the same urgency grid, scored by
  expected surplus times a fill probability. Exactly symmetric between sides by
  construction; a buy at mispricing $+x$ and a sell at $-x$ receive identical
  probability.
\item \textbf{Jev-argmax}: the model's modal action.
\item \textbf{Jev-sample}: a draw from the model's distribution.
\end{itemize}

The two model arms read one response, so for a given observation they are one
API call and differ only in the decode rule.

\section{The model and its measurement properties}

The model is a commercially available typed-decision model that returns, for a
multiple-choice question, a selected option together with a probability over
all options. Three properties affect measurement and are reported because they
constrain what can be claimed.

\paragraph{Non-determinism.} Identical requests return different answers. Four
repetitions of one request returned urgency scores of $2.84$, $2.79$, $2.82$
and $2.74$ and buy probabilities of $0.98$, $0.99$, $0.99$, $0.99$. There is no
seed parameter. Run-to-run noise is roughly $\pm 0.05$ on a $0$--$4$ urgency
scale and $\pm 0.01$ on probabilities. \emph{Reproducibility therefore requires
the response archive, not merely the code}: a rerun without it does not
reproduce. All 118{,}968 archived responses are retained and every reported figure is
regenerable from them.

\paragraph{Quantisation.} Probabilities are returned to two decimal places, so
three-option distributions routinely sum to $0.99$ or $1.01$. We accept and
renormalise within tolerance.

\paragraph{Undocumented failure modes.} The published status codes are
incomplete; the service returns bare \texttt{5xx} from its edge under load. A
client written to the documentation alone will treat these as fatal.

\section{Experiment 1: wording, conviction, and a null}
\label{sec:wording}

\subsection{Domain wording induces a conviction asymmetry}

Our first criteria were written in ordinary domain language: \emph{buy} as ``the
good is underpriced\ldots you want to acquire a unit'', \emph{sell} as ``the
good is overpriced\ldots you want to dispose of a unit''. At equal absolute
mispricing the model placed $0.988$ on the profitable side when buying was
correct and $0.738$ when selling was correct, a gap of $0.250$, with the
missing mass on \texttt{pass}.

The gap survives a second price level ($+0.225$), reversing the option order
($+0.235$), and renaming the options neutrally ($+0.185$). It does not survive
rewriting the two criteria as exact structural mirrors---``value is
\emph{above} the price'' versus ``\emph{below} the price''---under neutral
instructions, where the gap is $+0.005$ (Figure~\ref{fig:wording}).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{../figures/fig2_wording.pdf}
\caption{Probability the model places on the profitable side, against
mispricing, under the two wordings. The model is symmetric when asked

Two points matter for practice. First, the modal action is \emph{correct on
both sides at every point tested}: a practitioner reading only the selected
option sees nothing wrong. Second, the wording that induces the skew is the
natural one; symmetry had to be engineered deliberately.

\subsection{It does not move pricing error}

We predicted this skew would slow price discovery after negative shocks. A
four-seed exploratory run supported it ($+0.63$, four of four seeds positive,
Holm $p=0.061$). We registered the test and ran it on twenty fresh seeds. It
failed: $-0.184$, 95\% CI $[-0.479, +0.111]$, seven of nineteen seeds positive,
one-sided $p=0.897$. The confirmatory interval excludes the exploratory
estimate. With $\mathrm{SE}=0.140$ the run had ample power to detect the
exploratory effect, so this is evidence of absence.

We report it as such. Wording skews what the model says; it does not, in this
market, change what prices do.

\section{Experiment 2: liquidity failure under modal decoding}
\label{sec:liquidity}

The null above was correct for the question it asked, and the question was
wrong. Root mean squared pricing error is defined only over periods in which
trade occurred, and so discards exactly the periods where the effect lives. In
one run, modal decoding produced zero trades in all three up-jump windows and
full trading in all three down-jump windows---270 trades in the run, none in
the 45 periods after a positive shock.

We therefore registered a new outcome, defined for every period:
$\mathrm{traded}(d)$, the share of the fifteen periods after a jump of
direction $d$ in which at least one trade occurred.

\paragraph{Unanimity is directional in the market.}
\label{par:two}
A static probe of the model, holding the book fixed at its pre-shock level,
returns agreement of $92\%$ after an up shock and $96\%$ after a down shock.
Taken at face value that inverts the result: more agreement on the side that
halts less. We initially took this to mean unanimity could not account for the
direction, and posited a second mechanism. That was wrong, and the error was
using a probe statistic as though it were a market statistic. The probe holds
the book frozen and sets the trader's signal equal to its own private value;
the market does neither.

Measured in the market, agreement is already directional
(Table~\ref{tab:flow}). In the first period after a shock, $90.0\%$ of traders
buy following an up shock, while after a down shock $59.4\%$ sell against
$34.6\%$ buying. Unanimity after an up shock is near-total; after a down shock
it is far weaker. That ordering matches the halt, and no second mechanism is
required.

The effect therefore has a single mechanism. Unanimity is near-total after up
shocks and materially weaker after down shocks, which sets both the magnitude
of the halt and which shock it strikes; and it dissolves with market size
exactly as Section~\ref{sec:size} predicts. The sampling arm exhibits weaker
correlation throughout, consistent with the size results.

\paragraph{What remains unexplained.} Why the arm is more decisive on the buy
side than the sell side at equal absolute mispricing---$90.0\%$ against
$59.4\%$ at the shock---is not established here. It is not reproduced by either
symmetric baseline, and it is not removed by the mirror wording of
Section~\ref{sec:wording}, which eliminates the \emph{stated} conviction
asymmetry entirely. Candidate explanations we cannot separate with this design
include the model itself, the option naming in the response schema, the
under-specified referent of ``the current market price'' in the instructions,
and the omission of inventory from the state. We flag it as the clearest target
for follow-up.

\paragraph{A control that does not quite hold.} NBR returns $+1.0$ points
($t=3.33$), small but distinguishable from zero, indicating a slight genuine
structural tilt in the market. It is $1/26$ of the effect under test, ZI is
clean ($-0.4$, $t=-0.22$), and it does not survive the per-size controls of
Section~\ref{sec:size}. We report it because the registration required controls
to sit on zero and this one does not.

\section{Experiment 3: the mechanism, tested where it could fail}
\label{sec:size}

If the market halts because every trader reaches the same conclusion, the
effect must depend on the number of traders. Taking the agreement rate
measured in the market at the shock, $p \approx 0.90$ after an up shock
(Section~\ref{par:two}; the static probe gives a similar $0.92$, but we use the
market figure because the probe is not a market statistic), the probability
that all $N$ agree is approximately $p^N$, falling from $0.66$ at $N=4$ to
$0.03$ at $N=32$. We registered two
predictions before running: that the argmax gap would decay with $N$ (E1), and
that the sampling gap would not (E2).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{../figures/fig1_market_size.pdf}
\caption{The liquidity gap against market size, twenty seeds per point, bars
are standard errors. At $N=32$ both symmetric baselines trade in $100\%$ of
periods with a zero gap, so there is no structural floor and the residual
$4.2$ points is attributable to the arm.}
\label{fig:size}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Statistics.} The sweep reuses the same twenty seeds at every market
size, so pooling all eighty observations into one regression would treat
repeated measures as independent. We estimate a slope within each seed and test
over seeds. We note also that the $N=8$ argmax cell is the same data as D1.

\paragraph{E1 confirmed.} The gap falls from $37.0$ points at $N=4$ to $4.2$ at
$N=32$: slope $-0.112$ per doubling, seed-paired $t=-7.78$, nineteen of twenty
seed slopes negative. (The pooled estimate, $t=-6.91$, understates it.) It is
robust to dropping the $N=4$ point, which we flagged in advance as
floor-constrained because the symmetric baselines themselves trade in only
$33\%$ of periods there.

\paragraph{E2 not resolved.} We predicted sampling would show no decay. The
seed-paired estimate is $-0.016$ per doubling, $t=-2.00$, two-sided $p=0.060$,
with thirteen of twenty seed slopes negative. That does not falsify E2 at
conventional levels. It also does not confirm it: no equivalence margin was
preregistered, so a non-significant slope is not evidence of no slope. An
earlier draft of this paper reported E2 as falsified on the pooled $t=-2.53$;
that was an artefact of ignoring the repeated-measures structure, and we
withdraw it.

What can be said is weaker and still useful. Sampling's gap is small at every
size ($4.3$, $3.1$, $-0.1$, $0.0$ points) and its slope is roughly a seventh of
the argmax slope, though the ratio rests on a marginally estimated denominator.
Sampling does not abolish correlation; whether it decays with size on the same
curve is unresolved here.

\section{Discussion}

The practical claim is narrow and, we think, general in shape: \emph{the best
way to use one model is not the best way to use many of them}. Modal decoding
selects the most probable action for each agent and is collectively degenerate
when agents share information and require counterparties. Sampling selects a
less probable action some of the time; we did not measure per-agent trading
surplus, so we do not claim it is individually worse in payoff terms. Here it
was the difference between a market that cleared and one that did not. We do
not claim it is the only remedy---any source of decision diversity might serve.

The failure is largely not a reasoning failure. At the shock the modal action
takes the correct side in both directions, and across our static probe grid it
is correct at every mispricing. The loss occurs in aggregation, and evaluation
of a single agent's decisions cannot detect it. We do not claim the judgement
is flawless: $34.6\%$ of decisions at a negative shock are buys at a mispricing
of roughly ten ticks, which is the residual discussed below.

\section{Limitations}

\paragraph{The asymmetry in initial agreement.} Section~\ref{par:two}
establishes that agreement at the shock is directional---$90.0\%$ take the same
side after a positive shock against $59.4\%$ after a negative one---and that
this accounts for which shock the halt strikes. It does not establish why the
arm is so much more decisive buying than selling at comparable mispricing.

Two candidate explanations are not reproduced. It does not appear in the
symmetric baselines at any market size, though a market feature could in
principle produce an asymmetry for one policy and not another. And it is not
removed by the question wording. Under the mirror phrasing of
Section~\ref{sec:wording}, which eliminates the stated conviction asymmetry
($+0.005$ against $+0.250$), agreement at the shock is
$89.8\%$ after a positive shock against $64.8\%$ selling after a negative one.
Mirror wording is if anything \emph{more} willing to sell at the shock than
domain wording ($64.8\%$ against $59.4\%$), and the up-side figure is unmoved.
Whatever produces the asymmetry survives making the question symmetric.

A third candidate---the option naming in the response schema---is also ruled
out by measurement. Our mirror wording places the above-price description on
\texttt{option\_a}, which maps to buy, so any preference for the first option
would appear as exactly this bias. We asked the same 60 registered post-shock
states under two arrangements differing only in which label carried which
description. \textbf{All 60/60 states selected the same economic meaning under
both}, so the paired shift is exactly zero and equivalence holds within any
positive margin (G1). Option naming is not the residual, and the mirror control
used in Section~\ref{sec:wording} is not contaminated by it.

On the earlier, non-registered sample this comparison gave $48/50$ with a
$+4.0$ point shift---in the opposite direction to a first-option preference.
The corrected figure is stronger, and both are recorded.

What remains is the model itself, the task framing, or the state
representation, which this design cannot separate. We flag it as the clearest
target for follow-up work.

\paragraph{One model.} All results are for a single typed-decision model. We
have no evidence about others, and cannot say whether the agreement rate that
drives the mechanism is characteristic or idiosyncratic.

\paragraph{Provenance of the probabilities.} We treat the returned
probabilities as the model's beliefs. We have asked the vendor whether they are
calibrated posterior mass or post-processed, and have no answer. If the latter,
Experiment~1 is a statement about a serving stack rather than a model.

\paragraph{Full information only.} Every trader observes $F_t$ exactly, which
maximises the correlation the mechanism exploits. The delayed and noisy
treatments are implemented but unreported, and would plausibly weaken the
effect.

\paragraph{Scale.} Markets of four to thirty-two traders over 160 periods. The
extrapolation to larger markets is the fitted decay, not a measurement.

\paragraph{The estimand is memoised, and this narrows the claim.}
\label{sec:estimand}
Requests are
content-addressed: when two traders render the same tick-rounded state, the
second receives the stored response rather than a fresh call. Because the
service is non-deterministic, that is not the same system as $N$ agents each
calling it independently.

What we measure is therefore \emph{one archived response per distinct state,
reused across agents and periods}, and memoisation can only increase
within-state agreement.

\paragraph{How much it matters, measured.} Rather than leave this as a caveat
we measured it, on registered tests that could only hurt the result. We drew 60
distinct states from the registered population---those rendered inside the
fifteen-period post-shock windows of the confirmatory \texttt{jev\_argmax}
runs, seeds 24--43---and called each one hundred times live, bypassing the
cache. Every raw response is archived.

\textbf{F1 fails: the modal policy is not certifiably stable.} At $R=5$ the
proportion of fully stable states was $0.950$ with a lower confidence bound of
$0.887$, below the registered $0.95$. At $R=100$ the picture sharpens: $51$ of
$60$ states are perfectly stable, but nine are not, and four substantially so,
with modal shares between $0.76$ and $0.86$. We do not claim the modal policy
is effectively deterministic, because it is not.

\textbf{The claim-aligned quantity is counterparty availability, and
memoisation is not immaterial.} An earlier draft bounded the expected
\emph{majority share} at $+2.84$ points and compared it to the $25.8$-point
liquidity gap; external review correctly objected that these are
incommensurable, since the mechanism turns on whether \emph{any} trader takes
the opposite side---$1-\sum_a p_a^8$, which is nonlinear in the share. That
comparison, and the sentence it supported, are withdrawn.

Measured on the aligned outcome, with a Jeffreys posterior over each state's
action distribution:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
& Memoised & Independent & Counterparty gain \\
\midrule
All states ($n=60$)        & $1.000$ & $0.899$ & $+10.1$pp \\
After up shocks ($n=30$)   & $1.000$ & $0.899$ & $+10.1$pp \\
After down shocks ($n=30$) & $1.000$ & $0.899$ & $+10.1$pp \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Independent calling would supply at least one counterparty in roughly $10\%$ of
post-shock states that memoisation renders unanimous (one-sided 95\% upper
bound $14.4$ points; about $3.8$ points of the $10.1$ is residual posterior
width at $R=100$, the rest observed instability). Two consequences follow.

First, the headline liquidity gap of $25.8$ points is a property of the
\emph{memoised} regime, and under fully independent calling it would plausibly
be smaller. We report it as such; the dynamic experiment---replaying the
market with independent draws throughout---remains unrun.

Second, and usefully, \textbf{memoisation cannot explain the direction of the
effect}: the counterparty gain is balanced to a tenth of a point across shock
directions ($10.1$ against $10.1$), arising from different compositions of
unstable states on each side. Whatever unanimity memoisation adds, it adds
symmetrically, so the up-versus-down halt asymmetry survives it.

\textbf{Option naming is equivalent on its registered estimand.} G1 was
registered on returned probability mass and first implemented, wrongly, as a
test of the modal choice. Reanalysed on mass over the same 60 states: swapping
the labels shifts $P(\text{buy})$ by $+0.33$ points, 90\% CI
$[-0.33, +1.00]$, TOST $p<0.0001$ against a $\pm 10$-point margin. Modal
agreement was $60/60$, reported as secondary. The first analysis had also
silently pooled these states with an earlier, wrongly sampled population; the
figures above are restricted to the registered one.

The full deviation history of these tests---wrong population, wrong statistic,
point-estimate decisions, and the realignment---is in the preregistration
(sections 10f--10g), with the flawed runs retained as exploratory rather than
deleted. The sampling arm remains uncharacterised under repeated responses.

\section{Reproducibility}

Because the model is non-deterministic and has no seed, code alone does not
reproduce these numbers. The repository retains two response stores: the
content-addressed cache of unique request/response entries used by every
market run, and the raw repeated-call records of the estimand test, stored
separately because they deliberately bypass the cache. The analysis can be
replayed against the cache with live calls disabled, in which case a cache
miss raises rather than silently contacting the API.

The archive holds $118{,}968$ \emph{unique} cached request/response entries, one per distinct
request. That is a lower bound on live calls rather than a count of them:
retries, repeated uncached requests and overwrites of the same key would not
appear separately. The 300 deliberately uncached calls of the estimand test are
archived separately, in full, as \texttt{data/raw\_repeats.json}. Estimated
spend from the archived token counts is
\$$3.85$, consistent with the provider's reported balance. These figures, and
SHA-256 hashes of every result file and archive bundle, are generated from disk
into \texttt{data/manifest.json} rather than transcribed.

The preregistrations, including the predictions that failed and the reasons the
outcome measure changed between Experiments~1 and~2, are in the repository with
their commit timestamps.
